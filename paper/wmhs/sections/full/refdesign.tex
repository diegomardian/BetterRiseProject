\section{What the screen sees depends on the reference and the design}
\label{sec:matrix}

Whether an estimator reproduces the reference is not a property of the
estimator. It is a property of the estimator, the reference and the trial
design together, and changing any one of the three changes the verdict.
This does not follow from the algebra alone, and it is the part a
practitioner cannot guess, so we report it in full rather than by example.

The sweep crosses 14 estimators, three designs, two realised references, six
cohort sizes and six seeds (settings in Appendix~\ref{app:methods}).
Table~\ref{tab:matrix} shows nine of those estimators under two of the
designs; the five logistic-propensity variants appear in the regularisation
paragraph below and the third design, independent Bernoulli assignment, in the
paragraph after that. Under confounded
assignment, OLS has maximum residual $0.0663$ against the standardised
reference at $n=5{,}000$, but $3.5\times10^{-14}$ against the
variance-weighted reference equal to OLS by the Frisch--Waugh identity.
G-computation reverses the comparison: $0$ and $0.0663$, respectively.
Both consistently estimate the requested homogeneous effect.

With complete, stratified permuted blocks and 1:1 allocation, empirical
propensities are $0.5$ in every stratum. The two references coincide, and
OLS and even the unadjusted contrast reproduce them to numerical precision
($1.6\times10^{-14}$ and $6.7\times10^{-15}$). Independent Bernoulli
assignment at probability $0.5$ does not enforce this realised balance:
at $n=5{,}000$, OLS and the unadjusted contrast have maximum residuals
$9.51\times10^{-4}$ and $0.562$, respectively, against the standardised
reference. Thus 1:1 randomisation in expectation is insufficient for the
identity. Non-cross-fitted AIPW with
saturated nuisances also equals standardisation: its within-stratum
augmentation sums vanish (residual $4.4\times10^{-15}$ in the confounded
design). Exact matching using all controls within each stratum agrees to
$8.9\times10^{-16}$; using 20 or one control gives residuals of $0.052$ and
$0.164$. These are properties of the implemented estimator, reference and
design together. Appendix~\ref{app:matrix} also examines regularisation and
effect heterogeneity.

\paragraph{Why a learned patient model makes this harder, not easier.}
Our generators are parametric, and that is the favourable case. When the
generator is a closed form, the realised reference is too: $T(D)$ can be
written down, pre-specified, and checked against the estimator by inspection,
which is how the identities in Table~\ref{tab:matrix} were found. A learned
generative patient model removes all three affordances at once. There is no
closed form to inspect; the realised effect in a synthetic cohort is not
available analytically but must itself be \emph{estimated} from the sampled
patients; and the natural estimator for it --- compute the effect on the
cohort you just generated --- is exactly the estimator under test. The
functional reuse this paper describes is then the default wiring rather than
an oversight, and it arrives without an algebraic identity anyone could have
spotted in the code.

Two consequences follow, and we state them as predictions the audit makes
rather than as results, because we have not run them. First, the equality is
likely to become \emph{approximate}: a learned generator and a re-fit outcome
model share most but not all of their inputs, so the residual would sit near
zero rather than at it --- the regime \S\ref{sec:blind} shows the boolean
screen cannot see, and the one the residual scale $\rho$ exists for. A
practitioner who runs only \texttt{allclose} would find nothing. Second, step 1
of the audit inverts in difficulty: pre-specifying $T(D)$ is trivial for a
stratified simulator and is the substantive modelling decision for a
generative one, since it forces an explicit statement of what the synthetic
cohort's effect \emph{is} before an estimator is allowed to define it. We
think that is where the audit earns its keep, and it is the experiment we would
run next.

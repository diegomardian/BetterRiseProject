\section{The recovery curve could not see the estimator}
\label{sec:blind}

An earlier internal report validated the cutpoint on real held-out cells and
concluded it was sound. The statistic it used was a recovery curve: generate
samples with known composition and a known shift, run the estimator, plot
recovered against true. Ours ran between 1.00 and 1.07 wherever the rule said
``estimate'' on the reference draw --- 0.86 to 1.18 on the pooled one --- and
tightened with $n$ on both (Appendix Fig.~\ref{fig:generator}). That is what a
working estimator looks like.

It is also what this one looks like with the estimator divided out. The
estimator in that arm applies Equation~\ref{eq:kitagawa} to the sample's
\emph{own} realised summary statistics, so it reproduces them exactly. Across
all 1{,}711{,}200 replicates of four sweeps on two cohorts,
$\max|\hat{\imath} - i_{\text{realised}}| = 0$. The ratio is therefore
$i_{\text{realised}} / i_{\text{parametric}}$: what the generator drew over what
it was asked for. The estimator does not appear in it.

\paragraph{The general form.} Let $\theta$ be the parameter a simulator is asked
for, $\theta_{\text{realised}}$ the value the draw actually took --- which
differs from $\theta$ by sampling alone --- and $\hat\theta$ the estimator's
output. A recovery curve plots $\hat\theta / \theta$. Now suppose the estimator
is a deterministic function of the same sufficient statistics the generator used
to construct the draw. Then $\hat\theta = \theta_{\text{realised}}$
\emph{identically}, not approximately, and
\begin{mdframed}[linewidth=0.5pt,innertopmargin=4pt,innerbottommargin=4pt]
\[
\frac{\hat\theta}{\theta} \;=\; \frac{\theta_{\text{realised}}}{\theta}
\]
\vspace{-1.2em}
\begin{center}
\texttt{assert not np.allclose(est - truth\_realised, 0.0)}
\end{center}
\end{mdframed}
in which the estimator does not appear. The curve is a diagnostic of the
generator's sampling behaviour. It will sit near 1, tighten with $n$, and look
exactly like a validated estimator, because sampling error does shrink with $n$
--- and it would do all of that if the estimator were replaced by any other
estimator with the same property.

\iffull
The condition is not that the estimator is wrong, and not that it is right. It
is that the estimator and the generator share sufficient statistics. That is a
property of the plumbing rather than of the model, so it survives code review,
leaves no trace in the curve, and does not announce itself in any residual.
\else
The condition is not that the estimator is wrong. It is that the estimator and
the generator share sufficient statistics --- a property of the plumbing, so it
survives code review and leaves no trace in the curve.
\fi

\paragraph{Why this is the default, not an exotic case.} Oracle arms exist
precisely so the estimator sees clean inputs, and the cleanest available input is
the summary the generator just computed. Wiring the estimator to the generator's
own stratum means is the shortest path to an arm that runs, and it is the first
arm most pipelines get. This is not a strawman we built to knock down: it is the
arm we shipped.

\paragraph{What this looks like in a trial simulator.} Nothing above is about
cells. Take an external control arm drawn from a patient simulator: the generator
samples covariates, assigns outcomes with a specified mean per stratum, and you
ask it for a treatment effect $\theta$. You estimate that effect by
standardisation over the same strata --- G-computation \citep{robins1986}, which is
Equation~\ref{eq:kitagawa}'s identity under another name, and the estimator that
target trial emulation \citep{hernan2016} rests on. If the estimator standardises over
the strata the generator sampled within, weighting the stratum means the draw
\emph{realised}, it returns $\theta_{\text{realised}}$ and the recovery curve is
the generator's sampling error and nothing else. It will sit near 1, tighten with
cohort size, and certify nothing about the estimator. The same holds whenever a
virtual control arm's outcome model \emph{is} the generator's outcome model,
which is the natural first thing to build and the natural first thing to
validate this way.

\paragraph{So we built one.} Describing the fix is not demonstrating it, so
Figure~\ref{fig:trial} runs that simulator: three covariate strata, treatment
assigned with a stratum-dependent probability so the strata confound, a
requested effect of $\theta = 3$, and five estimators of it over cohorts from
100 to 5{,}000 patients.

Four of the five sit near 1 and tighten with cohort size --- the picture of a
validated estimator. Two of those four are exactly blind. G-computation over
the generator's own strata returns the realised effect with
$\max|\hat\theta - \theta_{\text{realised}}| = 0$, as the algebra says it must.
\textbf{So does inverse-probability weighting with a saturated propensity
model.} That these coincide is not news --- with the propensity estimated
nonparametrically per stratum, IPW is the same functional of the same
statistics as standardisation, and any reader of the causal literature knows
it. The news is that the coincidence is invisible where people look for it.
The two are different methods from different literatures written in different
code; nothing in either implementation says they are the same estimator, and
their recovery curves agree to machine precision, so the curve does not say so
either. We added this arm expecting a non-degenerate comparison, which is
precisely the mistake the column prevents and reading the code did not.
Whether an estimator shares sufficient statistics with the generator
is not visible in the code. It is visible in one column.

Cross-fitting \citep{chernozhukov2018} breaks it. Fit the propensity on one fold and
apply it to the
other, so the weights on a patient come from data that patient is not in, and
the estimator becomes a different functional: still consistent, still sitting
near 1, but now with a residual that is non-zero at every cohort size and shrinks
as the estimate improves. That curve is about the estimator. The unadjusted
difference is there to show the curve is not useless --- it ignores the strata,
so it is confounded, and the curve catches it immediately at a ratio near 2.4.

\iffull
\textbf{Nothing has to be held out for the check to stay silent, though.} Cross-fitting
breaks the degeneracy by splitting the sample, which leaves the reading that the
residual merely notices folds. It does not. Ordinary least squares of the
outcome on treatment and additive stratum dummies --- the estimator most people
would actually write --- sees every record and splits nothing, and is correctly
specified here, since the effect is additive and homogeneous. It is still not the
generator's functional: standardisation weights each stratum by its prevalence
and OLS weights it by the within-stratum treatment variance
$n_g\,p_g(1 - p_g)$, and the propensities differ across strata. Its residual is
non-zero at every cohort size, and \emph{smaller} than cross-fitted IPW's, so
the non-degenerate comparison here is not the weaker estimator. What the check
detects is a shared functional, not a shared sample --- and the two estimators
that look like careful causal inference are the blind ones.
\fi

The lesson is not that oracle arms are bad. It is that \emph{near 1 and
tightening} is compatible with the estimator having been divided out, and that
the residual against realised truth is what separates the two.

\iffull
\paragraph{The check is sound and not complete, and we can say how incomplete.}
An exact zero proves blindness: if $\hat\theta \equiv \theta_{\text{realised}}$
the ratio is a functional of the generator, by algebra. The converse fails, in
three ways we can measure (Table~\ref{tab:matrix}, 14 estimators $\times$ 3
designs $\times$ 2 realised truths $\times$ 6 cohort sizes $\times$ 6 seeds).

\textbf{Degeneracy belongs to the \emph{pair}, not the estimator.} OLS on
stratum dummies has residual $0.0663$ against the standardised realised truth
and reads informative. Against the variance-weighted realised truth --- OLS's
own finite-sample estimand, exact by Frisch--Waugh --- it is
$3.5\times10^{-14}$, exactly blind. The swap runs both ways: G-computation is
$0$ against the first and $0.0663$ against the second. Our Table~\ref{tab:trial}
reports OLS as the row that sees the estimator; it is, relative to a truth OLS
was never estimating.

\textbf{The verdict flips with the trial design.} Under stratified
permuted-block randomisation with 1:1 allocation --- what ICH E9 trials
actually run --- propensities are $0.5$ by construction, the two estimands
coincide, and everything except cross-fitted IPW and finite-ratio matching sits
at machine zero: OLS $1.6\times10^{-14}$, and even the unadjusted difference
$6.7\times10^{-15}$. No estimator changed.

\textbf{Two more estimators are secretly the same functional.} AIPW with
saturated nuisances and no cross-fitting is $4.4\times10^{-15}$: the
augmentation term is identically zero within each stratum, so the modern
doubly-robust estimator collapses onto standardisation, and \textbf{cross-fitting
is the only thing standing between it and a vacuous curve.} Exact matching on
stratum is $8.9\times10^{-16}$ with all controls, $0.052$ at 20 and $0.164$ at
1 --- blindness set by a matching ratio nobody thinks of as a validation
choice. IPW with an $L_2$-penalised logistic propensity runs $0.403$, $0.045$,
$0.0103$, $0.010$ as $C$ goes $0.1, 1, 100, 10^6$, and $1.3\times10^{-6}$ once
the solver tolerance is tightened: five orders of magnitude of apparent
informativeness from a regularisation constant.

\paragraph{So report a ratio, not a boolean.} Put the residual in units of the
generator's own sampling error,
\[
\rho \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{med}\,|\hat\theta - \theta_{\text{realised}}|}
                {\mathrm{med}\,|\theta_{\text{realised}} - \theta|},
\]
the fraction of the recovery curve's spread the estimator is responsible for at
all. OLS scores $\rho$ between $0.129$ and $0.137$ at every cohort size from
100 to 5{,}000, with no trend in $n$: \textbf{88\% of its recovery curve is generator
sampling noise}, and it passes the boolean check. Cross-fitted IPW falls
$1.69 \rightarrow 0.23$, ending 81\% generator noise at $n = 5{,}000$. The
unadjusted difference scores $52.8$ and is the only estimator here whose curve
is mostly about itself. $\rho = 0$ is the boolean check as a special case;
$0 < \rho \ll 1$ is the regime it cannot see.
\fi

\iffull
\paragraph{Censoring silences the check, and the fix is which truth you compare
to.} Time-to-event is the endpoint clinical trial simulation actually reports,
so we repeated the experiment with exponential times, confounded assignment and
administrative censoring (4 censoring levels $\times$ 7 cohort sizes $\times$
1{,}200 replicates $\times$ 6 seeds). The blind estimator is the per-stratum
exponential MLE, whose sufficient statistics --- events and exposure per cell
--- are exactly the generator's, and stay so under censoring.

\textbf{Against the observed data the check never misses}: $\max|\hat\theta -
\theta_{\text{realised}}^{\text{obs}}| = 0$ in all 28 cells. \textbf{Against the
latent event times it degrades to nothing}: $0$, $0.061$, $0.144$, $0.376$ at
$0\%$, $12\%$, $49\%$ and $78\%$ censoring. The estimator is unchanged; the
residual now measures what censoring hid. And a simulator author logs the
latent times \emph{because the generator knows them} --- so the natural
comparison is the wrong one, and it gets worse exactly as the design gets more
realistic.

By $49\%$ censoring the ordering inverts in 5 of 7 cohort sizes and by $78\%$
in 6 of 7, the blind estimator's latent-truth residual exceeding stratified
Cox's ($0.376$ against $0.293$ at $n = 3{,}000$); per replicate the inversion
rate is $0.53$--$0.61$. Not a clean reversal, and we do not claim one: a
practitioner ranking estimators by this column at high censoring picks the
blind one \emph{more often than not}. \textbf{The rule is to evaluate realised
truth on the observed data.} The latent truth is more precise, more available,
and wrong for this purpose.
\fi

\paragraph{The one-line check.} Compare the estimate against the \emph{realised}
truth rather than the parametric truth, and test whether the difference is
identically zero --- the assertion boxed above. If it is zero, the recovery
curve cannot see the estimator, and the arm needs a generator whose realised
summary statistics the estimator does not receive.
Ours was zero everywhere. In our case the curve takes the closed form $1/(1-s)$
exactly at the abstention point --- $5.000$, $2.000$ and $1.333$ at the three
tested shifts, with zero variance across replicates --- a constant, read at the
time as a two-fold over-estimate in the dangerous direction.

\iffull
The statistic is not worthless: it still catches an estimator that fails to
reproduce its input, and the design ships a broken-estimator control returning
$0.0$ for which the ratio comes out zero. It simply could not certify the arm it
was being used to certify.
\else
The statistic is not worthless --- it still catches an estimator that fails to
reproduce its input. It simply could not certify the arm it was being used to
certify.
\fi

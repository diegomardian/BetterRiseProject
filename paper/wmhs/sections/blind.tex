\section{The recovery curve could not see the estimator}
\label{sec:blind}

An earlier internal report validated the cutpoint on real held-out cells and
concluded it was sound. The statistic it used was a recovery curve: generate
samples with known composition and a known shift, run the estimator, plot
recovered against true. Ours ran between 1.00 and 1.07 wherever the rule said
``estimate'' on the reference draw --- 0.86 to 1.18 on the pooled one --- and
tightened with $n$ on both (Appendix Fig.~\ref{fig:generator}). That is what a
working estimator looks like.

It is also what this one looks like with the estimator divided out. The
estimator in that arm applies Equation~\ref{eq:kitagawa} to the sample's
\emph{own} realised summary statistics, so it reproduces them exactly. Across
all 119{,}600 replicates of the committed sweep --- and all 736{,}000 of a
second sweep at 500 replicates ---
$\max|\hat{\imath} - i_{\text{realised}}| = 0$. The ratio is therefore
$i_{\text{realised}} / i_{\text{parametric}}$: what the generator drew over what
it was asked for. The estimator does not appear in it.

\paragraph{The general form.} Let $\theta$ be the parameter a simulator is asked
for, $\theta_{\text{realised}}$ the value the draw actually took --- which
differs from $\theta$ by sampling alone --- and $\hat\theta$ the estimator's
output. A recovery curve plots $\hat\theta / \theta$. Now suppose the estimator
is a deterministic function of the same sufficient statistics the generator used
to construct the draw. Then $\hat\theta = \theta_{\text{realised}}$
\emph{identically}, not approximately, and
\begin{mdframed}[linewidth=0.5pt,innertopmargin=4pt,innerbottommargin=4pt]
\[
\frac{\hat\theta}{\theta} \;=\; \frac{\theta_{\text{realised}}}{\theta}
\]
\vspace{-1.2em}
\begin{center}
\texttt{assert not np.allclose(est - truth\_realised, 0.0)}
\end{center}
\end{mdframed}
in which the estimator does not appear. The curve is a diagnostic of the
generator's sampling behaviour. It will sit near 1, tighten with $n$, and look
exactly like a validated estimator, because sampling error does shrink with $n$
--- and it would do all of that if the estimator were replaced by any other
estimator with the same property.

\iffull
The condition is not that the estimator is wrong, and not that it is right. It
is that the estimator and the generator share sufficient statistics. That is a
property of the plumbing rather than of the model, so it survives code review,
leaves no trace in the curve, and does not announce itself in any residual.
\else
The condition is not that the estimator is wrong. It is that the estimator and
the generator share sufficient statistics --- a property of the plumbing, so it
survives code review and leaves no trace in the curve.
\fi

\paragraph{Why this is the default, not an exotic case.} Oracle arms exist
precisely so the estimator sees clean inputs, and the cleanest available input is
the summary the generator just computed. Wiring the estimator to the generator's
own stratum means is the shortest path to an arm that runs, and it is the first
arm most pipelines get. This is not a strawman we built to knock down: it is the
arm we shipped.

\paragraph{What this looks like in a trial simulator.} Nothing above is about
cells. Take an external control arm drawn from a patient simulator: the generator
samples covariates, assigns outcomes with a specified mean per stratum, and you
ask it for a treatment effect $\theta$. You estimate that effect by
standardisation over the same strata --- G-computation, which is
Equation~\ref{eq:kitagawa}'s identity under another name, and the estimator that
target trial emulation \citep{hernan2016} rests on. If the estimator standardises over
the strata the generator sampled within, weighting the stratum means the draw
\emph{realised}, it returns $\theta_{\text{realised}}$ and the recovery curve is
the generator's sampling error and nothing else. It will sit near 1, tighten with
cohort size, and certify nothing about the estimator. The same holds whenever a
virtual control arm's outcome model \emph{is} the generator's outcome model,
which is the natural first thing to build and the natural first thing to
validate this way.

\paragraph{So we built one.} Describing the fix is not demonstrating it, so
Figure~\ref{fig:trial} runs that simulator: three covariate strata, treatment
assigned with a stratum-dependent probability so the strata confound, a
requested effect of $\theta = 3$, and four estimators of it over cohorts from
100 to 5{,}000 patients.

Three of the four sit near 1 and tighten with cohort size --- the picture of a
validated estimator. Two of those three are exactly blind. G-computation over
the generator's own strata returns the realised effect with
$\max|\hat\theta - \theta_{\text{realised}}| = 0$, as the algebra says it must.
\textbf{So does inverse-probability weighting with a saturated propensity
model}, which is a different method from a different literature written in
different code, and which we added expecting it to be the non-degenerate
comparison. With the propensity estimated per stratum it is the same functional
of the same statistics, and its recovery curve is G-computation's to machine
precision. Whether an estimator shares sufficient statistics with the generator
is not visible in the code. It is visible in one column.

Cross-fitting breaks it. Fit the propensity on one fold and apply it to the
other, so the weights on a patient come from data that patient is not in, and
the estimator becomes a different functional: still consistent, still sitting
near 1, but now with a residual that is non-zero at every cohort size and shrinks
as the estimate improves. That curve is about the estimator. The unadjusted
difference is there to show the curve is not useless --- it ignores the strata,
so it is confounded, and the curve catches it immediately at a ratio near 2.4.

The lesson is not that oracle arms are bad. It is that \emph{near 1 and
tightening} is compatible with the estimator having been divided out, and that
the residual against realised truth is what separates the two.

\paragraph{The one-line check.} Compare the estimate against the \emph{realised}
truth rather than the parametric truth, and test whether the difference is
identically zero --- the assertion boxed above. If it is zero, the recovery
curve cannot see the estimator, and the arm needs a generator whose realised
summary statistics the estimator does not receive.
Ours was zero everywhere. In our case the curve takes the closed form $1/(1-s)$
exactly at the abstention point --- $5.000$, $2.000$ and $1.333$ at the three
tested shifts, with zero variance across replicates --- a constant, read at the
time as a two-fold over-estimate in the dangerous direction.

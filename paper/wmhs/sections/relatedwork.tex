\paragraph{Related work: validating a method against a simulator.} Evaluating a
statistical method by simulating from a known truth and measuring recovery is
long-standing practice with a written methodology behind it. Burton et
al.~\citep{burton2006} set out how such a study should be designed and
protocolled; ADEMP \citep{morris2019} gives the structure now in general use,
and names the data-generating mechanism and the methods under evaluation as
\emph{separate} items --- precisely so that the second can be assessed against
the first. That separation is exactly the property our arm lost, and the
guidance is right. What it does not supply is a test. It tells you the
generating mechanism should not coincide with the analysis model; it does not
tell you how to find out whether yours does, and in our case reading the code
would not have told us either (\S\ref{sec:blind}).

The case we describe is the degenerate end of that advice. It is not a
diagnostic weakened because the generator and the estimator share assumptions.
It is a diagnostic in which the estimator is \emph{algebraically absent} --- the
recovery ratio is a function of the generator alone, so the curve is unchanged
if the estimator is replaced by any other with the same property. That is
checkable in one line on the sweep you already have, and our contribution is
that line and the demonstration that two estimators from different literatures
can both fail it.

\paragraph{Related work: the abstention rule.} Cell-count filters of this shape are standard rather
than novel. Pseudobulk pipelines drop samples below a minimum cell count
\citep{crowell2020}, and differential-abundance methods --- scCODA
\citep{buettner2021}, Milo \citep{dann2022}, propeller \citep{phipson2022},
DA-seq \citep{zhao2021} --- test composition without an expression arm, so they
never face this estimand. cacoa \citep{petukhov2022} is closest: it separates
compositional from expression shifts explicitly. Our claim is not the filter. It
is that the filter is a selective-prediction rule \citep{chow1970,elyaniv2010,
geifman2017} and is therefore something you can \emph{calibrate} and get wrong,
and that it should emit a third verdict rather than silently dropping rows ---
because a dropped row and a row estimated at zero are indistinguishable
downstream.

\begin{abstract}
Simulate with known truth, run the estimator, plot recovered against true: this
is how virtual control arms and in-silico trial pipelines are validated. We show
the curve can be structurally incapable of seeing the estimator. If the
estimator is a deterministic function of the sufficient statistics the generator
drew, it cancels identically and the ratio becomes realised over requested truth
--- a property of the generator alone. That congenial methods are \emph{favoured}
is known; the degenerate case is not a magnitude but an identity, and that
decides how you find it. A favoured method draws a curve that looks
suspiciously good; a cancelled one draws a curve that looks exactly right, near
1 and tightening with $n$. \textbf{A validation that fails loudly is a nuisance;
one that succeeds silently is a hazard.} One column separates them: compare
against the \emph{realised} draw and test whether the difference is identically
zero. Ours was, across 1.7M runs. In a patient simulator it catches
G-computation, saturated-propensity weighting and non-cross-fitted AIPW --- so
cross-fitting is what stands between the modern doubly-robust estimator and a
vacuous curve --- while under the block randomisation real trials use, the
verdict flips and even an unadjusted difference goes blind. The check is sound
and not complete: an estimator can pass it with 88\% of its curve still
generator noise, so report the residual in units of the generator's own
sampling error. Under censoring it silences against the latent event times a
simulator naturally logs, and holds against the observed data. The abstention
rule our blind curve had certified does not survive recalibration.
\end{abstract}

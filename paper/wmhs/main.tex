\begin{abstract}
Simulate with known truth, run the estimator, plot recovered against true. This
is how virtual control arms, patient-level simulators and in-silico trial
pipelines are validated. We show the curve can be structurally incapable of
seeing the estimator. If the estimator is a deterministic function of the
sufficient statistics the generator drew, it cancels: the ratio reduces to
realised truth over requested truth, a property of the generator alone. The
curve still sits near 1 and still tightens with $n$, so it looks like a
validated estimator. One line detects it --- compare against the \emph{realised}
truth and test whether the difference is identically zero. Ours was, across
4{,}400 runs. What that curve certified was a selective-prediction rule for a
cell-type-resolved measurement: a standardisation decomposition, the identity
behind target trial emulation, given a third output \notest{} for the case where
no cell of the relevant type survives and the estimand has no referent. The
blind curve reported the rule validated. Calibrated properly against its own
pre-registered criteria the rule fails outright on one draw pool, returning no
cutpoint at any tested count; on the other it returns $ok = 90$ against a
committed 50. The previously reported 120 was an artifact of a grid carrying no
point in the interval where the criteria actually cross. A synthetic benchmark
then locates
what the rule was actually buying: three ungated methods invent an effect in
$200/200$ replicates where the estimand does not exist, but a width gate
suppresses that as completely as our count gate while costing no detections. The
finiteness check, not the threshold, does the work.
\end{abstract}

\section{Setup: a decomposition that answers when it should not}
\label{sec:intro}

A tissue model reports a differentiation marker down 90\% between a patient's
healthy and diseased sample. Two mechanisms produce that observation and they
imply different interventions. Either the cells making the marker have left, a
\emph{compositional} change, or those cells remain and produce less, an
\emph{intrinsic} change. Bulk measurement cannot separate them. Single-cell
measurement can, but only where enough of the relevant cells survive in both
arms. The separating estimator is Kitagawa's demographic standardisation
\citep{kitagawa1955}. Write $f$ for the fraction of cells of the type in
question and $m$ for their mean per-cell output:
\begin{equation}
\underbrace{(f_T - f_N)\, m_N}_{\text{compositional}} \;+\;
\underbrace{f_N\, (m_T - m_N)}_{\text{intrinsic}} \;+\;
\underbrace{(f_T - f_N)(m_T - m_N)}_{\text{interaction}}
\;=\; f_T m_T - f_N m_N .
\label{eq:kitagawa}
\end{equation}
Economists know this as Oaxaca--Blinder \citep{oaxaca1973,blinder1973},
epidemiologists as standardisation \citep{kline2011}. The same identity sits
under synthetic control arms \citep{abadie2010} and target trial emulation
\citep{hernan2016}. Any system reporting a group-resolved quantity inherits its
identifiability problem.

The problem is that Equation~\ref{eq:kitagawa} returns a number for any input.
The intrinsic term is a difference of two means over cells of one type. When the
diseased arm holds none of those cells, ``mean output per surviving cell of this
type'' has no referent, and the estimator still returns a number. So our
estimator returns one of three verdicts: an estimate, a wide-interval estimate,
or \notest{}, where the term is \texttt{None} and never $0.0$. Zero and ``we did
not ask'' are values a downstream average cannot tell apart. The rule gating
those verdicts is a cutpoint on $n$, the count of relevant cells in the diseased
arm: answer at $n \geq 50$, flag a wide interval from 20 to 49, abstain below 20.

\paragraph{Related work.} Cell-count filters of this shape are standard rather
than novel. Pseudobulk pipelines drop samples below a minimum cell count
\citep{crowell2020}, and differential-abundance methods --- scCODA
\citep{buettner2021}, Milo \citep{dann2022}, propeller \citep{phipson2022},
DA-seq \citep{zhao2021} --- test composition without an expression arm, so they
never face this estimand. cacoa \citep{petukhov2022} is closest: it separates
compositional from expression shifts explicitly. Our claim is not the filter. It
is that the filter is a selective-prediction rule \citep{chow1970,elyaniv2010,
geifman2017} and is therefore something you can \emph{calibrate} and get wrong,
and that it should emit a third verdict rather than silently dropping rows ---
because a dropped row and a row estimated at zero are indistinguishable
downstream.

\section{What abstention buys, and which part of it}
\label{sec:bench}

We measured what alternatives do where the estimand does not exist, on synthetic
cells whose truth is available in closed form (Appendix~\ref{app:bench}). Mature
cells draw Poisson; immature cells express nothing; one world sets the diseased
mature fraction to zero.

\small
\centering
\caption{Per-world refusals over 200 replicates, for three variants of identical

\label{tab:bench}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
\toprule
& \textbf{count gate (ours)} & \textbf{width gate} & \textbf{no gate} \\
\midrule
\texttt{annihilated} --- estimand undefined & $200/200$ & $200/200$ & $0/200$ \\
\texttt{depleted\_wide} --- $\sim$20 mature cells & $88/200$ & $0/200$ & $0/200$ \\
four other worlds & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
\midrule
false confidence & $0.00$ & $0.00$ & $1.00$ \\

\bottomrule

Ungated methods invent large effects. On a scale where the reference per-cell
mean is 20.0, the ungated ablation and pseudobulk differential expression each
invent a median $|$intrinsic$|$ of 7.99, and a naive difference of arm means
reports the entire 20.0-point drop as silencing in cells that are not there. We
include that last one to fix the scale, not as a serious competitor: nobody
proposes it as an intrinsic-effect estimator. Composition-only methods score no
false confidence for a different reason again --- they have no intrinsic arm at
all, which is inapplicability rather than caution.

\paragraph{The result that went against us.} The fair competitor is the width
gate, and it does the job as well as ours for free. It suppresses false
confidence completely, like our count gate, but costs no detections. The width
criterion never binds: in \texttt{depleted\_wide} the widest interval over 200
replicates is 1.081 against a detectable effect of 3.967, a factor of 3.7 clear.
So the width gate reduces to its \emph{finiteness guard} --- and that guard
alone is enough. \textbf{On this benchmark the only part of any gate that does
work is the check that the estimand exists at all.} Our count cutpoint's extra
88 refusals buy nothing measurable. Its slightly lower median error is a
selection effect, not an accuracy gain: it drops the noisy low-$n$ cases.
\S\ref{sec:calibration} finds the same cutpoint unsupported by a second and
independent route.

\paragraph{The finiteness guard is one line, and easy to omit.} With no mature
cell in an arm the standard error is undefined, and \texttt{nan > threshold} is
\texttt{False} in numpy and in pandas. A width gate written the obvious way
therefore \emph{declines to abstain} in exactly the world the estimand does not
exist. We check finiteness before comparing, which makes this competitor
stronger than the version most people would write. It is also the same coercion
we later found inside our own calibration routine (\S\ref{sec:blind}), arriving
there as a boolean in a rate instead of a number in a column.

\paragraph{Why a count cutpoint at all, rather than conformal prediction?}
Conformal methods give distribution-free coverage for a target that exists but
is uncertain \citep{vovk2005,angelopoulos2023}. The failure here is prior to
that: the parameter has no referent, so there is nothing to cover, and an
exchangeability argument over calibration data cannot manufacture one. Existence
and uncertainty are different questions, and the results above suggest we
answered the first one with a tool sized for the second. Conformal intervals are
the right instrument for the \emph{wide} verdict, where the estimand exists and
the interval is the whole question. We do not use them there yet.

\section{The recovery curve could not see the estimator}
\label{sec:blind}

An earlier internal report validated the cutpoint on real held-out cells and
concluded it was sound. The statistic it used was a recovery curve: generate
samples with known composition and a known shift, run the estimator, plot
recovered against true. Ours sat between 0.95 and 1.07 wherever the rule said
``estimate'' (Appendix Fig.~\ref{fig:generator}). That is what a working
estimator looks like.

It is also what this one looks like with the estimator divided out. The
estimator in that arm applies Equation~\ref{eq:kitagawa} to the sample's
\emph{own} realised summary statistics, so it reproduces them exactly. Across
all 4{,}400 rows, $\max|\hat{\imath} - i_{\text{realised}}| = 0$. The ratio is
therefore $i_{\text{realised}} / i_{\text{parametric}}$: what the generator drew
over what it was asked for. The estimator does not appear in it.

\paragraph{The general form.} Let $\theta$ be the parameter a simulator is asked
for, $\theta_{\text{realised}}$ the value the draw actually took, and
$\hat\theta$ the estimator's output. A recovery curve plots
$\hat\theta / \theta$. If the estimator is a deterministic function of the same
sufficient statistics the generator used to build the draw, then
$\hat\theta = \theta_{\text{realised}}$ identically, and
\[
\frac{\hat\theta}{\theta} \;=\; \frac{\theta_{\text{realised}}}{\theta},
\]
which contains no estimator. The curve is a diagnostic of the generator's
sampling behaviour. It sits near 1. It tightens with $n$, because sampling error
does. It would do both if the estimator were replaced by any other estimator
with the same property.

This is not exotic. Oracle arms exist so the estimator sees clean inputs, and
the cleanest available input is the summary the generator just computed. The
condition is a property of the plumbing, not of the model, so it survives code
review and leaves no trace in the curve. \textbf{One line detects it:} compare
the estimate against the \emph{realised} truth rather than the parametric truth,
and assert the difference is not identically zero. Ours was zero everywhere. In
our case the curve took the closed form $1/(1-s)$ scaled by the
realised-over-requested mature fraction, with zero variance across all 50
replicates at each shift --- a constant, read at the time as a two-fold
over-estimate in the dangerous direction. The statistic is not worthless: it
still catches an estimator that fails to reproduce its input, and the design
ships a broken-estimator control returning $0.0$ for which the ratio comes out
zero. It simply could not certify the arm it was being used to certify.

\section{What the blind curve hid}
\label{sec:calibration}

Three acceptance criteria were fixed in a design document before any sweep ran:
a detectable halving of per-cell output, $s = 0.5$; interval coverage of 90\%;
and discrimination of 80\%, the probability the interval excludes zero at that
effect. The cutpoints follow mechanically --- the smallest $n$ meeting both
criteria, and the smallest meeting coverage alone. The pre-registration is
mechanical rather than documentary: the panel, the labelling axes and the
falsification rule are frozen in a configuration file with a
\texttt{frozen\_on} stamp and mirrored by an exact-value assertion in the test
suite, so continuous integration fails if anyone edits one without the other.

Run properly on real held-out cells, the committed cutpoint of 50 does not come
back (Figure~\ref{fig:calibration}). The generator filters eligibility on
patient alone, so both synthetic arms draw from the held-out patients' cells of
\emph{both} tissues pooled. The design document lists what stays fixed and never
mentions tissue. Nobody made that choice; an implementation detail made it, and
it changes the answer. So we report both readings rather than the one that
yields a cutpoint. Pooled, the routine returns no cutpoint at all: discrimination
never reaches the 0.80 target in any count bin, topping out at 0.78 across 13
seeds at 50 replicates and at 0.75 across 8 seeds at 500. Restricted to the
reference tissue it does return one, and that is where the second lesson of this
paper repeats itself.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig1_calibration.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{The committed cutpoint is not what the pre-registered procedure
returns, under either reading of an unspecified design choice.} Dashed lines mark
the targets fixed before the sweep. Shading marks the committed rule's three
states. \emph{Left:} with the draw pool our sweep code implements, the routine
returns no cutpoint at any tested count. \emph{Right:} under a second reading of
the same unspecified choice it does return one --- $ok = 90$ once the grid
carries points in the interval where the criteria cross, against a perfectly
stable 100 when it does not. The committed value is 50 in both panels.}
\label{fig:calibration}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{The grid was reporting its own resolution.} Our committed grid varies
a mature \emph{fraction} against a fixed 2{,}000 cells, so the counts it can
reach are $\{0, 20, 40, 100, 200, 400, 800\}$ --- nothing between 40 and 100. On
the reference pool it returns $ok = 100$ on every one of the eight seeds at 500
replicates, and on twelve of thirteen at 50, and that stability is what made the
number look like a measurement. We added nine
fractions inside the gap, reaching 50, 60, 70, 80, 90, 110, 120, 130 and 150,
and changed nothing else. \textbf{The answer moves to 90}, and the crossing it
was hiding becomes legible: median discrimination runs $0.728$ at 45 mature
cells, $0.790$ at 65 and $0.858$ at 90, so the pre-registered target of $0.80$
falls between 65 and 90 --- inside the interval the committed grid could not
reach. Seven of eight seeds return $ok = 90$, $wide = 45$; the eighth returns 65,
which is where discrimination sits at $0.790$ against a $0.80$ target. Against
committed values of 50 and 20, the calibrated rule is roughly twice as strict on
both cutpoints. The pooled draw returns no cutpoint under either grid.

\textbf{The coarse grid was not measuring where the criteria first hold. It was
reporting the first point it happened to carry past them}, and reporting it with
a stability that came from having nowhere else to land. At 50 replicates the
filled grid does not resolve either --- the returned value wanders between 45 and
90 with the seed --- so the number in this paragraph rests on the replicate count
as much as on the grid, with the caveat Appendix~\ref{app:limitations} attaches
to it.

This is the same failure as \S\ref{sec:blind} in a second statistic. A recovery
curve that cannot see the estimator still draws a smooth line; a grid that
cannot resolve a crossing still returns a stable number. Both look like
measurements, and in both cases the fix was to ask what the statistic was a
function of rather than to look harder at its output.

Two further caveats belong with the number. The calibration
table's lowest bin merged replicates holding \emph{no} interval --- the
abstention case --- with those holding one. Comparisons against \texttt{NaN}
return false, so undefined intervals scored as coverage \emph{and}
discrimination failures, and the reported row read $(0.36, 0.31)$ where the
defined replicates give $(0.72, 0.62)$, exactly half. \textbf{An abstention
counted silently as a failure, inside the routine calibrating the rule built to
prevent exactly that.} And both rates were reported as bare point estimates; at
50 replicates the coverage crossing does not resolve
(Appendix~\ref{app:limitations}).

\paragraph{Where this leaves the rule.} Two independent routes now say the same
thing about the count cutpoint, and neither says what we expected. The benchmark
says its extra refusals buy nothing a finiteness check does not already buy. The
calibration says the value its own criteria return is 90, not the committed 50,
and that the previously reported 120 was a property of the grid. The
three-verdict output survives both. What does not survive is the committed
number, and the belief that either a recovery curve or a coarse grid had
confirmed it.

\paragraph{On real data, abstention is the modal outcome.} At the finest
annotation resolution, the one the compositional question is defined on, $28/28$
patients abstain under one pre-registered population definition and $24/28$
under the other. Both fail the pre-committed 50\% line. The specification
produces this, not the data: 5\% of a few hundred cells falls below a cutpoint of
20 by construction, so the resolution is defined at a granularity its own cohort
cannot support. At coarser resolutions the two definitions disagree and the
pre-registered response is \emph{not identifiable} rather than a choice --- all
six coarse combinations return that verdict, where a method forced to pick one
definition returns six confident numbers.

\section{Motivation: evaluate the reported uncertainty}
\label{sec:intro}

A nominal 95\% interval can appear to cover its reference almost perfectly
while covering the intended parameter less often. In our single-cell
simulation audit, changing only the scoring reference changes inclusion
from 98.9--99.6\% to 92.3--93.6\% at 800 cells. The source of the discrepancy
is explicit: the estimate and reference share a sampled mean, canceling
part of the error that the interval is meant to cover. We derive that
variance difference and examine its consequences for a reporting rule.

The case comes from our unpublished colorectal project, where point-estimate
recovery had been used to justify a 50-cell reporting boundary. We audit that
recorded inference using simulations resampled from observed single-cell
distributions. The analysis covers GUCA2A, one labeling axis and one weighting,
with ten SMC and six KUL3 patients. Each study draws cells from a two-patient
pool; repeated draws quantify performance conditional on those source pools,
not uncertainty over new patients. The contribution is this documented
failure, its quantitative explanation and paired interval comparisons.

Section~\ref{sec:calibration} assesses the original interval, a resampling-free
control and skew-aware alternatives at specified counts, including an
interior point of the flag-wide band. A short synthetic external-control
illustration (Section~\ref{sec:eca}) separates a related question: whether
the reference names the intended population. It supports the interpretation
of the audit rather than providing a second empirical case.

\paragraph{Relation to existing validation work.}
Simulation methodology already separates estimands, methods and performance
measures \citep{burton2006,morris2019}; ICH E9(R1) specifies the population
targeted by the clinical question \citep{ich2019}. Generator--analysis
alignment is studied through congeniality \citep{meng1994}, semi-synthetic
benchmarks \citep{franklin2014,curth2021}, interventional evaluation
\citep{gentzel2019}, and the inverse crime \citep{wirgin2004,kaipio2007}.
Generators can also disadvantage good estimators \citep{shaw2026}.
Simulation-based calibration checks Bayesian computation using
prior-predictive ranks \citep{talts2018}, with sensitivity depending on the
test quantities \citep{modrak2025}. Our frequentist assessment conditions
on empirical pools and specified effects; it applies established validation
principles to the claim made for this artifact.

\paragraph{Connection to patient simulation.}
Digital Twin Generators learn conditional clinical trajectories
\citep{alam2024}; ClinicalGAN evaluates personalized sequence generation
and trial-dropout prediction \citep{chandra2024}. Those modeling tasks differ
from assessing the coverage of a downstream treatment-effect interval.
Our audit addresses the latter validation layer, without evaluating either
system. A supporting learned-generator check shows why generator complexity
does not break an estimator--reference identity (Appendix~\ref{app:learned}).
Clinical transfer additionally requires source comparability, as in held-out
external-control validation \citep{ventz2019}, and an explicit causal design
\citep{hernan2016,schuler2022}. These are requirements for applying the audit's
lessons to patient world models, not results established by this study.

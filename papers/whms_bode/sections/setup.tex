\section{Motivation: validate the decision, not just recovery}
\label{sec:intro}

A clinical simulator may reproduce a treatment-effect reference accurately
while providing inadequate evidence for the decision it is meant to support.
The reference may average effects over the wrong population; even with the
right target, recovering a point estimate does not establish the coverage or
false-positive rate of the reported interval. Our thesis is that validation
must address the target, interval and reporting policy together. We examine
this gap through a constructed external-control example and a documented
single-cell self-audit, with different evidential roles.

The external-control example isolates a target-population error relevant to
single-arm trial simulation (Section~\ref{sec:eca}). A pooled-population
estimator appears exact against its observed-data reference yet overstates
the benefit among enrolled records. Changing the reference to their known
potential-outcome effect reverses the estimator ranking. This establishes a
possible failure and a diagnostic control in a fully specified synthetic
setting.

The empirical audit examines an actual validation claim in our unpublished
colorectal single-cell project (Section~\ref{sec:calibration}). Point-estimate
recovery had justified a 50-cell reporting boundary. Direct evaluation finds
excessive false positives with both percentile and Welch intervals, and
changing the scoring target exposes misleading apparent coverage. Controlled
grid comparisons document how the selected count depends on grid and binning
choices. Together, these analyses trace how a reassuring recovery result
can fail to justify the rule used downstream.

\paragraph{Contribution and prior work.}
The contribution is a reproducible worked audit, not a new validation
principle. Simulation-study methodology already separates estimands, methods
and performance measures \citep{burton2006,morris2019}; ICH E9(R1) requires
the clinical question to identify its target population \citep{ich2019}.
Generator--analysis alignment is studied through congeniality
\citep{meng1994}, plasmode and semi-synthetic benchmarks
\citep{franklin2014,curth2021}, interventional evaluation \citep{gentzel2019}
and the inverse crime \citep{wirgin2004,kaipio2007}; generators can also
disadvantage good estimators \citep{shaw2026}. Clinical external-control
validation already uses held-out studies to assess bias and type-I error
\citep{ventz2019}. Prognostic adjustment retains trial randomization
\citep{schuler2022}, and target-trial emulation makes the causal question and
design explicit \citep{hernan2016}. We apply these established distinctions
to an inspectable reporting-rule decision and a controlled population mismatch.
Simulation-based calibration (SBC) checks Bayesian computation using ranks
under prior-predictive simulation \citep{talts2018}; its sensitivity depends
on the test quantities \citep{modrak2025}. Our frequentist audit instead
measures coverage and rejection at specified effects and empirical source
pools, documenting where recovery was used to justify a reporting rule.
A supporting learned-generator check isolates why increasing generator
complexity does not break an estimator--reference identity
(Appendix~\ref{app:learned}).
Section~\ref{sec:validation} draws out the resulting checks for clinical
simulation; Appendix~\ref{app:relatedwork} provides further context.

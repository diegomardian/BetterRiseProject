\section{Clinical-simulation example: validating the wrong population}
\label{sec:eca}

Consider validating an estimator for a single-arm trial using an external
control arm (ECA). This constructed experiment isolates a mismatch between
the population named by the clinical question and the population used in
validation. The estimator and its observed-data reference deliberately
share the same population mistake; exact recovery may itself alert an analyst.
This is a pedagogical stress test, not a claim that the mistake is common.
A fixed synthetic registry supplies a single-arm trial and three external
controls per enrolled patient. Enrollment favors heavily pretreated strata;
induced benefits vary by stratum and latent individual responsiveness.
The intended target is the finite-cohort mean treatment effect among enrolled
records $E$, available to the simulator through both potential outcomes:
\begin{equation}
\tau_E=\frac{1}{|E|}\sum_{i\in E}\{Y_i(1)-Y_i(0)\},\qquad
\hat\tau(w)=\sum_g w_g(\bar Y_{1g}-\bar Y_{0g}).
\label{eq:ecatarget}
\end{equation}
Here $g$ indexes treatment-history strata, and the observed means use treated
enrollees and untreated external controls. Enrolled standardization uses
$w_g=n_{Eg}/|E|$; pooled standardization uses
$w_g=(n_{Eg}+n_{Cg})/(|E|+|C|)$ for controls $C$. Both use the same stratum
contrasts. Because effects vary across strata and enrollment changes their
mix, these weights answer different questions. Pooled standardization is
inappropriate for the stated enrolled target, not intrinsically invalid.

At 1{,}600 enrolled patients, pooled standardization has a median estimate of
1.890 outcome units while the enrolled records' median true effect is 1.003.
These medians fall on opposite sides of the prespecified illustrative threshold
of 1.5; this is not an estimated probability of a wrong decision.
Against the observed pooled contrast, the pooled estimator has zero error by
construction, while enrolled standardization appears inaccurate
(Table~\ref{tab:eca}). Using the enrolled potential-outcome reference reverses
that ranking. Merely using potential outcomes is insufficient: averaging them
over the pooled population still favors the estimator of the wrong target.
The target must specify both the effect and the population over which it is
averaged.

\paragraph{Controls distinguish agreement from target alignment.} At zero case-mix shift, pooled
standardization still reproduces its observed-data reference but its bias
against the enrolled effect is only $-0.002$ units. Equality therefore does
not establish that an estimate is wrong. Under the full shift, pooled
weighting balances the measured strata between arms to numerical precision
while their weighted distribution differs from the enrolled distribution
(maximum standardized difference 0.964). Between-arm balance does not establish
target-population alignment. At the smallest and largest trial sizes, root mean squared error against
$\tau_E$ is respectively 0.608 and 0.105 for enrolled standardization,
and 1.082 and 0.892 for pooled standardization. Increasing sample size therefore does not remove
the population mismatch in this experiment.

For clinical-simulation validation, the actionable check is to compare the
analysis population with the population named by the estimand, in addition
to checking between-arm balance. Potential-outcome access solves the missing
counterfactual problem inside this simulation; it does not choose the target
population. The next section examines the complementary problem: even a
correctly specified target requires direct evaluation of interval performance
and the reporting boundary.

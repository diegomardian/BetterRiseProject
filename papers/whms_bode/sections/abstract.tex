\begin{abstract}
Recovering a reference effect does not establish that a simulator supports
the intended decision. We examine this gap through a synthetic external-control
example and an empirical self-audit. In the former, using a reference for the
wrong population favors an estimator of the wrong effect; an enrolled-population
reference reverses that ranking. In the latter, we audit a 50-cell reporting
rule in two colorectal single-cell cohorts that had been justified by
point-estimate recovery. In 5{,}000 simulations per
setting, its nominal 95\% percentile intervals reject a true null in
9.8--32.2\% of studies at the reporting boundary. A paired comparison with
Welch intervals improves effect coverage but leaves null rejection at
8.9--30.8\%; substituting a Welch interval does not repair the rule. An exact variance calculation shows that, under a halving of expression,
the sampled reference removes three quarters of the reference-arm variance
contribution from the error being assessed; at 800 cells, apparent coverage
exceeds fixed-pool coverage by 5.8--6.8 percentage points. We also document
grid-dependent threshold selection. The practical lesson is to specify the target, test
null and alternative performance together, and evaluate an interval and its
reporting threshold as a combined rule.
\end{abstract}
